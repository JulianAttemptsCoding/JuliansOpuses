## Problem

Simulating a particle shower inside a calorimeter with [Geant4](https://geant4.web.cern.ch/) is accurate and slow: every secondary particle is tracked through the detector material. An analysis that needs millions of events pays that cost millions of times.

Fast-MC-CBSC is a learned surrogate. Given only the four-momentum of an incoming neutron, it generates the energy deposited in every readout channel directly. Making it fast turned out to be the easy part. What I still don’t know is whether the generated showers are close enough to Geant4 that a physics analysis wouldn’t notice the difference.

## Detector and data

The target is a zero-degree calorimeter with one electromagnetic layer followed by 64 hadronic layers: 65 layers and 6,790 channels in total.

The training set is 764,940 Geant4 events, converted into 187 content-addressed shards so that any result can be traced to the exact bytes it was computed from. Splits are audited for integrity, and the public visual-QA artifacts and checkpoint selection use zero test events. The primary result domain is 50–250 GeV.

## Model

A shower is sparse and highly structured. Most channels read exactly zero, and the total energy has to add up. Instead of asking one network to emit 6,790 numbers at once, the generator factorizes a shower into a cascade of decisions, each conditioned on the ones before it:

1. the total detector response for this neutron
2. which depths are occupied, and how much energy each layer receives
3. how many cells fire in each layer, and which ones
4. how a layer's energy is shared among those cells
5. a deterministic decoder that writes the result into 6,790 channels

The decoder guarantees exact zeros outside the selected cells and exact energy accounting. Both hold by construction. Neither one tells you whether the showers look like Geant4’s.

## Engineering and QA

Most of my time has gone into checking the model the way you would check an experiment, because it can be wrong without anything looking broken. The repository is CLI-first, with strict tensor and data contracts, deterministic evaluation and 740 source tests. The rule I wrote down for QA is that a passing check tells you about one artifact. It doesn’t mean the model is done.

Defects found and corrected along the way include tensor-shape errors, masked-softmax behavior, gradient accumulation, catastrophic forgetting, non-finite gradients, loss calibration, support constraints and data-split integrity. The project record documents eleven defects with how each was caught, and five diagnoses that were wrong and later corrected.

One example is the learning-rate schedule. The config declared a cosine schedule over the full run, but resuming from a checkpoint restored the scheduler state of an ancestor run, including its much shorter horizon. For its entire history the project had been annealing on a 12-epoch sawtooth rather than the schedule it declared, and nothing looked wrong because the config always stated the intended value.

## Speed

An early prototype generated detector showers roughly 200× faster than the Geant4 baseline in benchmarked runs. That speed-up is why the project exists. It says nothing about whether the showers are right.

Matching averages is not the same as matching the distribution. The stricter test is a classifier two-sample test: train a classifier to tell generated showers from Geant4 showers. If the generator were faithful, the classifier could do no better than chance, an AUROC of 0.5. For a generator, a lower score is the better one.

It does much better than chance. On the accepted best checkpoint the classifier reaches **AUROC 0.84 ± 0.01**, with per-energy-bin values from 0.77 to 0.92, against a target of 0.65. I have never gotten close to it. Validation loss kept improving during training and that number did not move, so I report a lower loss as progress in training and nothing more.
